**Confidence key**
- **H**: primary source read in full. None in this report, for the reason above.
- **M-p**: primary source seen only through search extracts. Examples: a Health Canada page, an SBD/RDS, the ODB formulary, a company release, a journal abstract.
- **M-s**: secondary or commercial source seen through search (news, law firm, price tracker, affiliate site).
- **U**: unverified. This includes background knowledge of trials and labels that was not re-checked in this session. Treat as "to be confirmed".

**Lawful supply route used throughout (U, general background; not re-checked this session).** A product with a DIN, prescribed by a licensed Ontario prescriber (physician or NP, within scope), dispensed by an OCP-accredited pharmacy. Products with no DIN can only be reached through Health Canada's Special Access Program (serious or life-threatening conditions where conventional therapy has failed or is unavailable; not body composition) or an authorised clinical trial (CTA). A prescription, a compounding pharmacy or a licence does not make an unauthorised active ingredient lawful (see Section 5).

## 1. Headline findings

1. **Generic semaglutide is authorised in Canada (2026).** Canada is the first G7 country to do so.
   - Dr. Reddy's (Ozempic-referencing, T2D): NOC **Apr 28, 2026**.
   - Apotex Apo-Semaglutide (Ozempic-referencing, T2D): NOC **May 1, 2026**.
   - Apotex **Sevmia** (Wegovy-referencing, **chronic weight management**, age 12+): NOC **Jun 29, 2026**. This is the first generic for weight.
   - Sandoz Semaglutide (Ozempic-referencing, T2D): NOC **Sep 18, 2026**.
   - Health Canada said **6 more submissions** were under review as of Jun 29, 2026. That count was 8 others at Apr 28. Vimy Pharma filed an ANDS in May 2026.
   - Novo Nordisk also holds two identical "second brands", authorised Dec 22, 2025: **Plosbrio** (= Ozempic) and **Poviztra** (= Wegovy). Their launch was pending as of the June 2026 IQVIA data. (M-p/M-s)
2. **Launch and price.**
   - The Ozempic-referencing generics launched in mid/late May 2026. Apotex said its price is about one-third of brand Ozempic.
   - Ontario Drug Benefit (ODB) lists Apo-Semaglutide and Dr. Reddy's pens at a **drug benefit price of $78.141 per pen**, against Ozempic at $227.94. The ministry pays $78.141 for Ozempic too, because they are interchangeable. That is about 66% below brand.
   - Reported cash prices are about $88–99 a month (Costco) and $100–125 a month (chains). Telehealth offers list about $149–150 a month. (M-p ODB; M-s retail)
   - **Dr. Reddy's commercial supply has been disrupted since July 2026** by an active-ingredient (API) batch problem. Its relaunch target was reported as March 2027.
   - **Sevmia**: no confirmed launch or retail price was found. The ODB lists it as "Off-Formulary Interchangeable" at $325.048 per 4 mg/3 mL pen, with no public payment. One extract mentions only a 1 mg-dose pen; **whether the 1.7 mg and 2.4 mg maintenance strengths are authorised is unverified**. Check the DPD. (M-s/U)
3. **Patent background.** Novo's core Canadian semaglutide patent (**CA 2,601,784**) lapsed after a missed maintenance fee (reported as about CAD 250, around 2019). CIPO said a lapsed patent cannot be revived. Regulatory data protection expired **Jan 4, 2026**, which opened generic entry. (M-s)
4. **Tirzepatide's weight indication is under the Zepbound brand, not Mounjaro.**
   - **Zepbound** KwikPen: NOC **May 13, 2025** for chronic weight management (adults; BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with a comorbidity). Launched Jul 9, 2025. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in adults with obesity added **June 2026**.
   - **Mounjaro** remains a T2D product: adults since Nov 24, 2022, plus ages 10–17 in 2026. Lilly Canada stated in Dec 2024 that tirzepatide was not then approved for weight management. (M-p)
5. **Not in Canada.**
   - No generic liraglutide was found in Canada.
   - Oral semaglutide 25 mg for obesity (FDA approved Dec 22, 2025) has no Canadian authorisation found.
   - Orforglipron (FDA approved Apr 1, 2026 as Foundayo) is **under Health Canada review** and not authorised.
   - Retatrutide, CagriSema, survodutide and MariTide are not approved anywhere. CagriSema's FDA decision is expected in Q4 2026.
6. **Peptides.** Health Canada says injectable peptides are regulated as prescription drugs and that "research use only" labelling gives no exemption.
   - The Apr 9, 2026 advisory names BPC-157, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, TB-500, GHK-Cu, MOTS-c, epitalon, melanotan I/II, retatrutide and others.
   - Health Canada has escalated: a Type I recall of BPC-157 and GHK-Cu **APIs sold to compounding pharmacies** (May 2026); a **permanent injunction** against Canlab Research (court order Jun 11, 2026); and **bacterial contamination findings** (Nov 6 and Nov 12, 2026).
   - None of the listed "commonly marketed peptides" has a DIN found. Tesamorelin (Egrifta) once had Canadian DINs, but **both are cancelled**.
7. **Compounding.** Health Canada's Jun 2, 2025 position treats compounded GLP-1s as **manufacturing, not compounding**, because they use unauthorised APIs and duplicate available products. A Calgary compounded semaglutide + B6 product was recalled (Type I, Jan 21, 2025). POL-0051 lists "producing an identical product that is already commercially available, unless there is a shortage" as an indicator of manufacturing.

## 5. Policy: compounding and manufacturing

**Health Canada POL-0051, Policy on Manufacturing and Compounding Drug Products in Canada** (PDF dated Jan 26, 2009, supersedes GUI-0030; HTML page live on canada.ca). Seen via search extracts only (M-p). It is a multi-factor test, and Health Canada weighs all the factors together:
- **Copies of commercially available products.** "Producing an identical product that is already commercially available, unless there is a shortage" is an **indicator of manufacturing**. A shortage of, or no supply of, a commercially available product is listed as a factor supporting compounding.
- **Ingredient source.** A compounded product "should be produced from an authorized drug or Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) used in an authorized drug for use in Canada or listed in a recognized Pharmacopoeia." Using an **unauthorised active ingredient** points to manufacturing.
- **Patient relationship.** Compounding must be "recommended by a health care practitioner, in the context of an individual patient-health care practitioner relationship". It must not be produced to sell to third parties who resell outside that relationship.
- **Imported APIs.** Under the Food and Drug Regulations, imported APIs for compounding must be for drugs "not commercially available" in Canada. Health Canada reads this to include discontinued or suspended drugs, drugs in shortage or back-order, dormant drugs, and approved but unmarketed drugs (Alberta College of Pharmacy summary, M-s).
- **Peptides page.** Health Canada's "Peptides: Regulations and enforcement" page (canada.ca) applies POL-0051 directly. Its extract reads: "Activities involving unauthorized peptide ingredients, or those that extend beyond the accepted scope of compounding, may be considered manufacturing rather than compounding. In these cases, Health Canada will take action under the Food and Drugs Act" (M-p).

**Health Canada position on compounded GLP-1s, Jun 2, 2025** (circulated through OCP, CPBC and ACP) (M-p):
- Health Canada considers production of "compounded" GLP-1 products **manufacturing rather than compounding**. Most of these products use **unauthorised APIs** (chemically synthesised semaglutide) and **duplicate currently available authorised products without unmet therapeutic need**.
- At that date no chemically synthesised semaglutide product had been authorised, and no recognised pharmacopoeial standard for semaglutide existed. Health Canada questioned the science of combining semaglutide with other ingredients (e.g. **pyridoxine/B6**).
- It allows for "unique case-by-case scenarios" only.
- It noted the activity was happening **in licensed pharmacies** and said it would proceed with **enforcement and compliance actions**.
- Caveat (U): 2026 generics may use synthetic API. Even so, compounding a copy of a now commercially available product remains a manufacturing indicator under POL-0051.

**Provincial and regulator statements (2023–2026)**
- **Ontario College of Pharmacists (OCP):**
  - "Important guidance to pharmacists during the current Ozempic shortage: expectations when compounding semaglutide preparations" (date not captured; written during the shortage). It requires contacting the prescriber to confirm a compounded alternative is appropriate. It warns about semaglutide salt forms (sodium, acetate), and states that calling a compounded preparation "Ozempic" is **misleading** (M-p extract).
  - OCP republished Health Canada's Jun 2, 2025 position (ocpinfo.com; PDF uploaded Jul 2025) (M-p).
  - In a 2026 CBC investigation, OCP said that compounding an NHP with ingredients unauthorised by Health Canada "could be investigated as a breach of professional conduct". CPSO said information physicians give about treatments must be accurate and evidence-informed (M-s, CBC summary).
- **Alberta College of Pharmacy**: "Unauthorized compounding of semaglutide-containing products is not permitted" (Jun 10, 2025) (M-s).
- **College of Pharmacists of BC**: bulletin reproducing the Health Canada position (Jun 2025) (M-p).
- **CPSA (Alberta physicians)**: "Unauthorized injectable peptides and their potential serious health risks" (date not captured) (M-s).
- **NAPRA**: **not found**. No NAPRA statement on compounded GLP-1s or peptides surfaced; the search budget ran out before a site-specific check.
- **CBC investigation (2026)**: doctors reportedly used a regulatory "loophole" (compounding, framed around NHP status) to prescribe BPC-157. Health Canada says promoting unauthorised products, including patient-specific compounded ones, is illegal. Injectables cannot be NHPs (Gowling WLG, M-s).

**Implication for the plan (analysis, not legal advice).** Compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide, and any compounded peptide whose API is not used in a Canadian-authorised drug or listed in a recognised pharmacopoeia, should be treated as unlawful manufacturing. A prescription, a compounding pharmacy, a pharmacy licence or a "research use only" label does not change that. Health Canada now recalls such APIs at the **pharmacy** level (RA-82047).
